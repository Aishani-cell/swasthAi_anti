Abstract.RuralhealthpodsspecificallyunderIndia’sSmartVillageini-
tiatives deploy use cases in smart healthcare, aligned with ITU-T Recom-
mendation Y.4218. These consist of diagnostic hardware like vital-sign
monitors, digital X-ray, portable CT, and blood-test machines. However,
these operate without on-site clinicians to interpret and evaluate the data
that is generated. We present SwasthAI, a privacy-preserving, neuro-
symbolic,multi-agentclinicaldecisionsupportsystem(CDSS)thatcloses
thisinterpretationgapwithoutlettingalargelanguagemodelreasonover
raw, unverified evidence. A multimodal pipeline first normalizes mixed
language symptom text, radiological images, respiratory acoustics, and
laboratory values. A deterministic rule engine then converts this evi-
dence into an immutable graph referenced to LOINC codes, with an
emergency circuit breaker that can bypass the LLM entirely for detect-
ing life-threatening patterns. Only certified evidence reaches a diagno-
sis agent and a downstream drug-recommendation agent both of whose
reasoning is based on retrieval augmented generation (RAG) Standard
Indian and International clinical guidelines. These agents are all locally
served to keep patient data on-site under low-connectivity conditions.
In a 50-case 25-category clinical vignette evaluation, the safety circuit-
breaker achieved 86.0% accuracy (macro-F1 72.55%) in gating cases,
the diagnosis agent reached 78.0% accuracy (macro-F1 63.44%), and the
drug-recommendation agent achieved 74.17% mean Jaccard similarity to
gold-standard drug classes. We describe the system’s ingestion, symbolic
verification, retrieval-grounded reasoning, and persistence layers, report
these preliminary results, and target possible preliminary deployment at
the Satnavari, Maharashtra (India’s first smart village).

1 Introduction
1.1 Motivation and Challenges
Motivation-Rural and remote healthcare (in India’s Smart Village Initiatives)
is limited less by diagnostic hardware than by a shortage of specialists to in-
terpret the results. This gap became concrete during a visit to Satnavari, Ma-
harashtra—India’s first smart village—where healthpods built under the ITU-T
Y.4218 smart-village healthcare framework house automated vital-sign moni-
tors, digital X-ray units, portable CT scanners, and blood-test machines, yet
operate without an on-site doctor [3]. Front-line workers (ASHAs, Anganwadi
Workers, ANMs, Telemedicine Technicians) can capture vitals and imaging, but
have no physician to interpret them and no prior patient record for context [9].
The ITU-T Y.4218-aligned hardware thus solves dataacquisition, not datain-
terpretation—motivating SwasthAI as a system to close this interpretation gap
while preserving patient privacy and maintaining longitudinal patient history.
Challenges—This goal is complicated by three open problems. First, large lan-
guage models reason fluently over clinical text but hallucinate findings and mis-
read numeric values, offering no guarantee of evidence-grounded diagnosis. Sec-
ond, symbolic rule-based systems avoid hallucination but cannot handle unstruc-
tured, multilingual, multimodal input without exhaustive manual rule author-
ing—leaving the fusion of LLM reasoning with deterministic auditability largely
unresolved. Third, cloud-dependent architectures conflict with data-protection
regulations like HIPAA and GDPR, since they transmit patient data off-site and
cannot function without reliable internet connection.[4].
1.2 Proposed Model and Contributions
Our model employs a four-stage evidence pipeline that standardizes heteroge-
neous inputs (multilingual symptoms, imaging, lung acoustics, lab panels) via
specialized agents into a unified, verified format and then fuses them with SQL-
retrieved patient records. It validates the result through a deterministic neuro-
symbolic safety layer that enforces physiological bounds, LOINC-mapped lab
resolution, and an emergency circuit breaker that bypasses generative reasoning
when needed, thus delivering four core contributions:
1.F our Stage Evidence Pipeline:normalizing multimodal clinical data be-
fore LLM inference,
2.Neuro-Symbolic Safety Layer:constraining generative outputs via de-
terministic rules and threshold alerts to prevent hallucinations,
3.Modular Multi-Agent Implementation:orchestrating symptom-NLP,
vision, sound, diagnosis, and drug agents in one pipeline, and
4.Longitudinal Evidence F etching:that preserves and contextually re-
trieves normalized patient history for new consultations.

2 Related Work and Vulnerabilities Addressed by
SwasthAI
Clinical AI has advanced along four largely fragmented fronts: multi-agent rea-
soning [9], safety guardrails [6], multimodal perception [1], and RAG. [1] Multi-
agent frameworks improve structured decision support and mitigate LLM hallu-
cinations [9,6], but rely on soft consistency checks rather than hard verification.
SwasthAI,ontheotherhand,enforcesdeterministicgatingofgenerativediagnos-
tic hypotheses against a clinical evidence graph and rule engine. Separately, local
inference safeguards privacy [5,8,10], while multimodal models interpret medical
imaging [1] and respiratory audio [10], still these capabilities remain isolated as
discrete modules rather than a unified evidence stream. SwasthAI consolidates
symptom, imaging, acoustic, laboratory, and vital signals within a single local,
RAG-grounded pipeline [1]. At scale, deployments such as India’s eSanjeevani
demonstrate the real-world utility of AI-CDSS [7], but edge-oriented architec-
tures combining multimodal extraction, deterministic validation, local RAG, and
longitudinal EHR persistence remain underexplored. This is a gap SwasthAI di-
rectly addresses as a privacy-preserving solution for resource-constrained health-
pods [2].

3.2 Multimodal Data Acquisition and Ingestion
SwasthAI ingests vitals, multilingual symptoms, radiological images, respiratory
audio, and laboratory reports. Patient authentication relies on hashed creden-
tials. Vitals are range-constrained and a rule-based triage layer delivers imme-
diate threshold feedback without LLM involvement.
Optional radiological imaging inputs (PNG/JPG/JPEG or DICOM) are pro-
cessed locally via Qwen3-VL:32B-Instruct, and respiratory recordings are classi-
fied using OPERA-CT. Laboratory reports, including PDFs and scanned docu-
ments, are parsed to extract structured test names, values, units, and reference
ranges. Missing modalities are explicitly logged rather than silently dropped. Op-
erators can review extracted values before transactional persistence to SQLite,
after which verified encounter data enters the downstream reasoning pipeline.
3.3 Privacy-Preserving Processing and Persistence
PrivacypreservationprecedesanyLLMinteraction.Identifyingattributes(name,
phone, DOB, address) pass through a dedicated scrubbing stage so that LLM
components see only the coarse demographics (age, sex, pregnancy status).All
LLM inference runs on locally hosted models within the deployment site rather
than external cloud APIs, adding a data-containment boundary against third-
party transmission and enabling operation under limited connectivity. These
measures are architecturally aligned with HIPAA and GDPR, and their data
minimization principles. Persistence, authentication, and longitudinal tracking
arehandledbyanormalizedSQLitelayerofseventables(patients,medical_history,
consultations,vitals,lab_results,imaging_scans,extracted_symptoms)
with active foreign-key constraints and cascading deletions; credentials and gov-
ernment identifiers (e.g., Phone no. hash) are stored only as one-way SHA-256
hashes.
3.4 Multimodal Evidence Extraction
Each modality is routed to a dedicated extraction agent that converts raw inputs
into normalized, structured evidence before deterministic validation and clinical
reasoning.

3.5 Laboratory V alue Resolution
Extracted laboratory data undergoes deterministic triplet verification—analyte
identity, unit consistency, and physiological plausibility, all prior to rule evalu-
ation. A curated reference database maps heterogeneous clinical aliases and re-
gionalreportingvariantstocanonicalnames,LOINCcodes,andsurvivalbounds.
Values failing validation are isolated rather than guessed to prevent error prop-
agation. Furthermore, a localqwen2.5:7bmodel provides advisory identity sug-
gestions for unrecognized tests without yielding generative control over physio-
logical thresholds.
3.6 Deterministic Symbolic Rule Engine
Vitals and resolved laboratory values are evaluated by a non-LLM rule engine us-
ing explicit, demographic-conditioned thresholds to produce structured flags and
a canonical evidence graph. Representative rules manage blood-pressure staging,
age-specific pulse bounds, and laboratory markers, while surfacing missing core
tests to prevent silent omissions. Critical anomalies trigger an emergency circuit
breaker to bypass generative reasoning, ensuring fully reproducible and auditable
safety gating.

4 Results and Discussion
4.1 Experimental Setup
The evaluation was performed on a set of clinical vignettes covering 25 distinct
diagnosticcategories(twocases per category)i.e N= 50. Each casecontained pa-
tient demographics, vital signs, and laboratory findings. Only the patient inputs
were passed to the pipeline and Gold labels were withheld from the pipeline
and used exclusively for post-hoc scoring. The emergency circuit breaker and
deterministic rule engine,Safety Status agent, gave two outputs-Approved(non
critical case) orRejected(critical case that should bypass LLMs). All agents- the
lab resolver agent, the diagnosis agent usingqwen2.5:14b, the drug recommen-
dation agent and symptom NLP agent usingqwen2.5:14b, except Safety Status
Agent, were run locally via Ollama(temperature=0.0) ontwo NVIDIA GeForce
GTX 1080 Ti GPUs (11 GB VRAM each).

4.6 Discussion
The diagnosis agent predicted 39 out of 50 cases correctly but it was unable
to diagnose some conditions (STEMI, UTI, DKA, COPD exacerbation, AKI,
and one of two dengue cases as shown in Fig 2). These errors likely stem from
symptoms overlapping, abnormal lab values and vital sign patterns that make
these conditions difficult to differentiate from others.
The Safety status agent demonstrated an accuracy of 86 percent. It correctly
classified 39 out of 42 non-critical cases as APPROVED. While 3 non-critical
were wrongly classified as emergency cases (false positives). Among the 8 cases
requiring emergency escalation, 4 were correctly identified and 4 were not (false
negatives). This is shown in Figure 3b. This shows that while the Safety State
agent was generally effective for most cases, it had lower sensitivity for detecting
all critical cases.

Another thing worth noting is that the proposed system’s 78.00% diagnosis
accuracy is measured by exact string matching against a gold label. Thus there
were a small number of cases where the diagnosis agent produced a clinically
equivalent but differently-phrased diagnosis (e.g. abbreviation vs. full name)
which were scored as incorrect under this metric. Due to time constraints, the
matching logic could not be extended to account for semantic equivalence as
well.
